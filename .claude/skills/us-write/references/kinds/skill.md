# Teaching a professional skill

**At the end of a chapter** the reader can do the practice on their own material and judge the result against named criteria: say what is weak in a piece of work, why it matters to the person it is for, and how to fix it. Knowing the rule is not enough; they must see its absence in a real draft.

**The usual arc.** A failure the practice exists to prevent, told as it happens (the test where five users "passed" a task because the wording gave the answer away) → why it goes wrong: the mechanism in people or organisations → a worked case: a weak version, a better one, and the reasons line by line → the judgement calls, where the rule bends and why → the reader does it on their own material, then checks it against a short list.

**The real thing** is a worked case: a weak version, then a better one, with the reasons. Weak advice: "Write task scenarios that don't lead the user." Better, a case:

> Weak task: "Use the Filter button to find vegan recipes under 30 minutes."
> Better: "You have half an hour and a vegan friend coming to dinner. Find something you could cook."

The reasons: the weak task names the control, so it tests reading, not finding; it has no motive, so the user cannot decide when they are done. The better one gives a goal and a stopping point in the user's own words. Then the edge case: when you *are* testing one control, how to name the goal without naming the control.

**Three exercise types.**
- *Judge it*: give three real-looking tasks (or emails, or interview questions) and ask which is weakest and why, against the chapter's criteria.
- *Rewrite it*: give a weak meeting agenda with no decisions named; the reader rewrites it, and the hidden answer shows one good version with reasons.
- *Do it on your own material*: "Write four tasks for a test of your own product; check each against the five questions in the chapter's list."

**Mark with a risk note** numbers presented as research findings ("five users find 85% of problems"), named methods and who devised them, rules from a law or standard (accessibility levels, employment law on interview questions, citation styles), and advice that is right in one culture or company size and wrong in another.

**Usual failures of generated material, and the cure.**
- *Advice with no worked case*: a list of tips. Every principle appears first inside a case, weak then better.
- *Platitudes* ("know your audience"): replace with what the better version visibly does that the weak one does not.
- *A forced program*: where there is nothing to run, the real thing is the case: set `run: none`. A skill that is practised with a computer (estimating a system's load, solving contest problems) does run things: set `run:` to its language and keep this shape, with the worked case carried by real programs. For problem solving the weak version is the slow sure solution, or the plausible wrong one that a small test breaks; the better one is the technique with the argument that it is right.
- *No judgement calls*: show one case where the rule should be broken, and why.
- *Practice nobody can check*: give a checklist and a model answer, so the reader can judge their own work.
